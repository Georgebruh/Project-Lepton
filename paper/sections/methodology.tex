\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

LEPTON has two layers that share one flood computation, which sets a storm's rain against flood
hazard maps and population counts to estimate how many residents of each barangay the rain
exposes. Layer~1 uses it, with past cases and rain, to forecast next week's cases in QC. Layer~2
turns it into prophylaxis stock for each barangay and health center. This section describes the
data, the shared computation, the two layers and how they were evaluated. It holds no formulas:
the formula behind each step is in Appendix~\ref{app:formulas}, and each assumption's value and
test in Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}.

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Cases}
QCESD's list gives each case's date of onset and date of admission, but not the date it was
reported. Cases were counted by the CDC/MMWR epidemiological week (Sunday to Saturday) of their
onset, from the week of 4--10 January 2015; the list's own morbidity-week column, whose meaning
changes between years, was not used. Cases reach hospitals days after onset, so a week's count is
final only once 17 days have passed since the week ended, the time within which 99\% of cases
were admitted. The last final week is 20--26 September 2026; later weeks are missing, not zero.
With the project adviser's approval, the list was used only as weekly counts, and no case-level
record appears in this paper or in the project's code repository \checkme{E2: name the ethics
board and the date of the ethics request or exemption}.

\paragraph{Rain}
Observed rain is NASA's IMERG Late daily product, version 07, on a 0.1\textdegree{} grid
\parencite{huffman2024}: the rain of each UTC day (8~AM to 8~AM Philippine time) from 1 June 2000
to 13 October 2026, over a box that holds QC and the catchments that drain into it. The
forecast runs use ECMWF's IFS forecasts at 0.25\textdegree{} from Open-Meteo's archive of past
runs \parencite{zippenfenig2023}, which gives, for every hour since February 2024, the value from
runs started at least one, two or more days earlier; one to five days were used. They are judged
against the ERA5
reanalysis \parencite{hersbach2020}, hourly rain from June 2000 to December 2025, also from
Open-Meteo. Both come from the four 0.25\textdegree{} cells that overlap the catchments, each
weighted by its area inside them, with each hour added to its UTC day.

\paragraph{Catchments}
Floods on the Marikina are driven by rain over its upper watershed \parencite{abon2011}, so a
storm's rain is measured over the catchments that drain into QC, not over the city. Six level-12
sub-basins of HydroBASINS \parencite{lehner2013} were kept: those upstream of a point on
the Marikina (the Sto.~Niño gauge) and on the Tullahan (Novaliches), traced through each
sub-basin's downstream link, and the San Juan River's own sub-basin, whose upstream trace would
wrongly add the whole Laguna de Bay basin.

\paragraph{Flood maps and people}
Project NOAH's 5-, 25- and 100-year flood hazard maps of Metro Manila \parencite{noah_nd} mark
three hazard levels: low (up to 0.5~m of water), medium (0.5 to 1.5~m) and high (over 1.5~m).
Their invalid shapes were repaired and, where two levels of one map overlap, the area was given
to the higher level. Residents come from WorldPop's 2024 population grid at about 100~m
\parencite{bondarenko2025}, fitted to the 2024 census totals of QC's 142 barangays
\parencite{psa_census2024}, within the barangay boundaries of the national mapping agency and the
Philippine Statistics Authority \parencite{namria_codab2025}. Ages come from the 2020 census by single year of age and sex
\parencite{psa_age2020}, and pregnancies from the 32,578 live births registered in 2024 to
mothers living in QC \parencite{psa_births2026}. Health centers and their locations come from
the DOH's National Health Facility Registry \parencite{doh_nhfr}.

\paragraph{Storms}
Layer~2 ran on the four storms in Table~\ref{tab:storms}; Section~\ref{sec:scope-scope} says why
Kristine was added to the three in the project proposal. For each, the city government's road and
evacuation updates and news reports gave the days QC was flooded and the barangays named as
flooded. A barangay counts only if a report from that storm names it as flooded, or its residents
as evacuated, rescued or left homeless; a road that shares a barangay's name does not count. The
project's repository links every fact to its source.

\paragraph{Benchmark data}
The reproduction of M3 was checked on the national weekly counts its authors used
\parencite{parreno2023}, from 2019 to 2023.

\subsection{Shared Flood Computation}\label{sec:flood}

\paragraph{Residents in each flood zone}
WorldPop says where people live within a barangay, and the census says how many: each barangay's
WorldPop counts were scaled so that they add up to its 2024 census total, splitting a pixel that
a boundary cuts by its area on each side. The residents who live in each zone were then counted
for each barangay, hazard level and map, a pixel half inside the zone counting half its
residents.
The result, 142 barangays by three hazard levels by three maps, serves both layers.

\paragraph{Storm rain and return levels}
Each UTC day's IMERG rain is averaged over the catchments, each 0.1\textdegree{} cell weighted by
its area inside them, and a storm is measured by its wettest three consecutive days. The project
proposal used the single wettest day, which missed the long habagat floods of 2025 and 2026, so
the single day is kept only as a comparison run. To place a rain total among the flood maps, a
Gumbel distribution was fitted by maximum likelihood \parencite{coles2001,virtanen2020} to each
year's
wettest three-day total, using only years before the forecast or storm year: 18 to 26 years,
depending on the year, never fewer than ten. The fit gives the three-day totals expected once in
5, 25 and 100 years, one for each NOAH map, and a no-flood floor $R_0$ at half the five-year
total, below which nobody counts as exposed. All of these are on IMERG's own scale. A second
comparison run refits them without IMERG's partial first year (June to December 2000).

\paragraph{Graded exposure and the exposure index}
A rain total between two of these levels exposes, in each barangay and hazard level, a number of
residents interpolated in a straight line between the two maps' counts: nobody at the floor, each
map's residents at its own return level, and the 100-year map's residents for any heavier rain.
The flood exposure index of a week is the percentage of QC's residents who live in zones that the
week's wettest three-day total would flood. Its denominator is the 2024 census total the
population map was fitted to, not that week's population, so the index is a pure share. Because
the return levels are refitted each year, the index for every week since 2015 is recomputed with
each forecast year's levels, and a forecast reads the series of its own year.

\subsection{Layer 1: City Forecast}\label{sec:layer1}

\paragraph{What each model may see}
At the end of each week, the forecast day, every model forecasts the number of cases with onset in
the next week. The study's analysis plan fixed in advance what to do with each kind of case data
that might arrive. For weekly counts by week of onset without report dates, the kind received, it
prescribes a delay guard: every model sees counts only up to two weeks before the forecast day, as if the last
two weeks had not been reported yet, so each forecast reaches three weeks past the newest count it
sees. Two weeks is an assumption, because QCESD's reporting delay is not known. Rain and the
exposure index are never hidden, since IMERG's Late run is published about 14 hours after each
day ends \parencite{huffman2024}. M4's recent-cases input is built
for every training week with the same three-week lag it has at the forecast, so the model never
learns from a fresher signal than it gets on the forecast day. A check overwrites every count a model may not see
with an impossible value and confirms that no forecast changes.

\paragraph{Models}
Four models are compared.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{M0}, last year: the count 52 weeks before the target week.
  \item \textbf{M1}, the seasonal average: the mean count of the same week of the year over the
    epidemic threshold's reference years (below).
  \item \textbf{M3}, the benchmark: the Prophet--XGBoost hybrid of \textcite{latigay2026}. Prophet
    \parencite{taylor2018} is fitted to the weekly counts with multiplicative seasonality, a
    changepoint prior scale of 0.55, weekly and yearly seasonality and no holidays. XGBoost
    \parencite{chen2016} then learns Prophet's in-sample errors from two features, Prophet's
    forecast and the ISO week number, and the forecast is Prophet's plus XGBoost's correction,
    floored at zero. The paper gives no XGBoost settings beyond ``low complexity'', so xgboost's
    defaults with 100 boosting rounds were used.
  \item \textbf{M4}, the proposed model: a negative binomial regression of the week's count on
    the season
    (three pairs of yearly sine and cosine waves), recent cases (the sum of the two newest counts
    the model may see), the last two weeks' rain over QC, the flood exposure index (the larger of
    the last two weeks' values) and an indicator for the COVID-19 years 2020--2021. QC's
    population enters as an offset, so the other terms describe cases per resident.
\end{itemize}

M4 follows the time-series regression design of Section~\ref{sec:background}, and its two weeks
of rain match the lag at which leptospirosis admissions in Manila peaked after rainfall
\parencite{matsushita2018}. Its rain over QC averages the IMERG cells, each weighted by the
WorldPop residents it holds inside the city, so rain where people live counts more; a week's
rain is the sum of its seven days. QC's population grows by a constant percentage between the
2015, 2020 and 2024 censuses, and at the 2020--2024 rate after mid-2024. M4 was fitted by maximum
likelihood with statsmodels' negative binomial regression, which estimates the overdispersion
along with the coefficients \parencite{seabold2010}. A term with the same value in every training
row cannot be estimated and is left out: this happened only to the COVID-19 indicator, on the 105
forecast days before any COVID-19 week reached the training data. Rain would have been dropped
had it correlated with the exposure index above 0.8 in a training set; that never happened. To
test the flood index, \emph{M4 without it} was also fitted on the same rows.

Before M3 was ported to QC, it was rebuilt on the benchmark's own data and evaluation: 260 national
weeks from 2019 to 2023, the paper's three-step filling of the weeks missing in 2020, its rolling
origin (a first training window of 115 weeks, forecasts two weeks ahead, the origin moved 16 weeks
at a time: nine folds and 18 forecast weeks) and its four scores, which are mean absolute error,
root mean squared error, symmetric mean absolute percentage error and bias. The reproduction was
to be accepted if Prophet's and the hybrid's mean absolute errors came within about 10\% of the
published ones; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reports the outcome. In the QC backtest, M3 is refitted with
the same settings at every forecast day, on the weeks the guard lets it see. QC's weekly counts
have no missing weeks, so its gap filling never applied.

\paragraph{Forecast ranges}
Every model gives a full forecast distribution: a negative binomial whose mean is the model's
forecast and whose spread makes its ranges as wide as the model's own past misses. The spread is
estimated from the model's last 104 forecasts outside the COVID-19 years whose target weeks the
guard already lets it see. Until a model has 26 such forecasts, which took the first 28 forecast
days (target weeks to 22--28 July 2018), the spread comes from its own training fit; for M3
that is Prophet's fit, because XGBoost repeats its training errors almost exactly and would make
the range far too narrow. Where past misses were no wider than a Poisson distribution allows, the
forecast is Poisson. Each distribution gives the expected cases, the 90\% range (from its 5th to
its 95th percentile) and the probability that the count breaches the epidemic threshold.

\paragraph{Epidemic threshold}
A week's threshold is the mean plus two standard deviations of the same week's counts over up to
the five most recent complete years before the target year, at least three, leaving out the
COVID-19 years; week 53 uses week 52's threshold. A year counts as complete only once the guard
lets a model see all its weeks. A week breaches when its final count is strictly greater than the
threshold, and the breach probability follows the same rule. In the first week of 2018 the last
week of 2017 was still hidden, leaving two reference years, so the backtest's first forecast day
is the second week of 2018, forecasting the week of 14--20 January.

\subsection{Layer 2: Barangay Prophylaxis Stock}\label{sec:layer2}

\paragraph{From rain to exposed people}
On observed rain, a storm's rain is its wettest three-day IMERG total over the catchments whose
last day falls during the flood, set against IMERG return levels fitted on the 24 to 26 years
before the storm's year. Graded exposure turns it into exposed residents per barangay and hazard
level, which are split into three groups by each barangay's shares: children under 18, from the
2020 census's age shares applied to 2024 totals; pregnant women, 0.75 times the city's 2024
births to resident mothers (24,434 women), split among barangays by their share of women aged
15--49; and adults, everyone else. Pregnant girls aged 15--17 move out of the child group, so no
one is counted twice and the three groups add up to the barangay's exposed residents. Everyone
living in a flooded zone counts as exposed, at any depth, and stock is reported at full coverage,
as if every exposed resident took prophylaxis; a health office scales the totals by the share it
expects to reach.

\paragraph{Risk tiers and stock}
Following the risk tiers of DM~\mbox{2023-0309} \parencite{doh2023}, a flood lasting two days or
more is treated as continuous exposure, which makes every exposed adult high risk. In a shorter
flood, a share $w$ of exposed adults, those with a wound or with floodwater in the eyes, nose or
mouth, are moderate risk and the rest low risk; $w$ is reported at 0, 0.5 and 1. Each tier's doxycycline regimen sets
its stock in 100~mg capsules: 2 for a low-risk adult (200~mg once), 10 for a moderate-risk adult
(200~mg a day for five days, the upper end of the memo's three to five) and 2 for each week of
flooding for a high-risk adult (200~mg once a week, a part week counted whole). Each exposed child
needs one course per week of flooding, at least one, which a pharmacy turns into tablets by
weight, and each exposed pregnant woman 6 amoxicillin 500~mg tablets (twice a day for three days).
These are stock-planning figures, not prescriptions: the memo requires a physician's consultation
before any prophylaxis.

\paragraph{Health centers}
A barangay's need goes to its health center. A barangay with several centers splits its need by
the residents who live nearest each, and one with none sends it to the nearest center, at most
2.04~km away. Of the registry's 69 QC facilities, the City Health Office is treated as holding
the city's stock rather than handing it out, a barangay health station registered 11~km from its
own barangay is left out, and a center listed twice is counted once, which leaves 66 health
centers.
When stock is short, each pool (adult doxycycline, children's courses and amoxicillin) is shared
high risk first, then moderate, then low: a tier is served in full while stock lasts, and the tier
the remaining stock cannot cover is shared in proportion to need.

\paragraph{Forecast-driven runs}
In use, a rain forecast one to three days ahead would trigger the calculation. This use was
replayed on the archived ECMWF forecasts issued one, two and three days before a storm's
three-day window. Each day of the window is taken from runs started that many days before the
window began, so every hour of a lead's window comes from a forecast at least that old. Each
lead takes the forecast's wettest three-day window among those ending on the storm's wettest
days (Table~\ref{tab:storms}), and the same lead's forecast for the observed run's window is
reported beside it. Forecast rain is set
against return levels fitted the same way on ERA5's three-day totals over the same years, never
against IMERG's: each product has its own scale, and ERA5 is made with an earlier version of
ECMWF's forecast model \parencite{hersbach2020}. A forecast cannot know how long the flood will
last, so each lead reports stock for a one-day flood, at each $w$, and for a seven-day flood.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\paragraph{Weekly backtest}
Layer~1 was tested in a rolling-origin backtest with one forecast day per week, from the second
week of 2018 to the week of 13--19 September 2026: 454 one-week-ahead forecasts per model, for the
weeks from 14--20 January 2018 to 20--26 September 2026. At every forecast day each model is
refitted on what the guard lets it see. The run stops if any model fails to forecast or returns a
missing value, so every model is scored on exactly the same weeks, against final counts. The
return levels behind the exposure index are refitted at the first forecast day of each year, on
the years before it.

\paragraph{Scores}
The primary endpoint, mean absolute error (MAE), needs no forecast spread, so it is scored over
the 349 forecasts whose target weeks fall in 2018--2019 and 2022--2026, leaving out the COVID-19
years. Root mean squared error, bias and the mean absolute scaled error (MASE) use the same weeks;
MASE divides each model's MAE by the average miss of ``same week last year'' over the target
weeks of 2016--2017, one fixed number for every model \parencite{hyndman2006}. Scores of the
forecast distribution must wait out the spread's warm-up, so they use the 247 forecasts for 2022
to 2026: the weighted interval score (WIS) over eleven central ranges and the median
\parencite{bracher2021}, how often the 50\% and 90\% ranges held the count, and, for the breach
probabilities, the Brier score \parencite{brier1950} and the Brier skill score against always
quoting the usual breach rate, the share of non-COVID weeks from 2018 up to the guard's cutoff
that breached. A reliability diagram drawn with the CORP method \parencite{dimitriadis2021} shows
whether the breach probabilities match how often breaches happened. Every score is also computed
over all 454 forecasts, COVID-19 years included, as a check (Section~\ref{sec:results-checks}).

\paragraph{Primary endpoint and tests}
Each week's difference is M4's absolute error minus M3's. The mean of these differences is the
difference in MAE: below zero, M4 misses less. Misses come in runs during outbreaks, so the weeks
are not independent, and a 95\% interval is set on the mean with a moving-block bootstrap
\parencite{kunsch1989}, resampling blocks of eight consecutive weeks 2,000 times with a fixed
seed, then again with 4- and 13-week blocks. Leaving out the COVID-19 years joins 2019's last
week to 2022's first, once in 349 weeks. The two-sided Diebold--Mariano test
\parencite{diebold1995} is reported beside the interval. The same bootstrap compares M4 with M4 without the
flood index, the flood test, and with M1, the practical test. Because rain was never dropped, M4
without the index is M4 minus one term on every forecast day; for such nested models the
Diebold--Mariano test is not valid \parencite{clark2001}, so the flood test reports only the
interval. The benchmark paper scored 18 forecast weeks with a one-sided test; this study scores
349 weekly forecasts with a two-sided interval, so the two sets of scores are not directly
comparable.

\paragraph{Alarms and outbreak episodes}
An alarm is raised when a forecast's breach probability reaches a cut-off, chosen for the best F1
score, the balance of precision and sensitivity, on the first half of the 2022--2026 forecasts and
then tested on the second half. Breach weeks at most two weeks apart form one outbreak episode, and
the number of episodes caught is reported with exact 95\% intervals \parencite{clopper1934}. An
episode's lead time runs from the forecast day of its first alarm, for one of its breach weeks, to
the week surveillance would have shown its first breach, which under the guard is two weeks after
that breach week. An episode is caught in advance when its lead time is at least one week.

\paragraph{Layer 2 storm runs}
Each storm runs on observed rain with its reported flood duration, then again with the
continuous-exposure cut-off at one and at three days, and with the duration one day shorter and
one day longer (only longer for Kristine's one-day flood). A barangay is flagged when at least 25\% of its residents live in zones the rain
floods; 50\% is also reported. Such a share is needed because every barangay has some residents
in the five-year zones, so any exposure at all would flag all 142 once the rain passed the floor
\checkme{the team confirms the 25\% and 50\% rule, Notes.md 1.2}. Each run gives two shares: the
share of flagged barangays that reports named as flooded, and the share of named barangays that
were flagged. The reports are incomplete and no barangay case counts were available, so these
comparisons show the chain at work; they do not validate it.

\paragraph{Software and checks}
Every step from the raw data files to each number and figure in this paper is a Python script in
the project's code repository, run with one command each in the order its README gives
\checkme{E10: the repository's public link}. Most downloads are scripted too, and every raw file
has a note saying where and when it was obtained. Package versions are pinned, and every setting
and assumption is held in one configuration file. Automated checks with answers known in advance guard
the steps: for example, M4 must recover known coefficients from 50 simulated data sets of 400
weeks, the Gumbel fit must recover known return levels, and the leakage check above must leave
every forecast unchanged.
