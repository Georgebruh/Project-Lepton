\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We report the city forecast first and the storm runs second. Every model forecast every one of
the 454 weeks of the backtest, so all models are scored on exactly the same weeks.

\subsection{Layer 1: City Forecast}\label{sec:results-layer1}

\paragraph{Primary endpoint}
Over the 349 forecast weeks outside the COVID-19 years, M4 missed the weekly count by 8.88
fewer cases than M3 on average (mean absolute errors of 6.70 and 15.59 cases a week; 95\%
moving-block bootstrap interval of the difference $-19.93$ to $-1.88$), so M4 is more accurate
than the benchmark. The interval stays below zero with 4-week blocks ($-18.40$ to $-2.50$) and
13-week blocks ($-20.94$ to $-1.31$), and the two-sided Diebold--Mariano test agrees
($p = 0.007$). Figure~\ref{fig:forecasts} shows both models' forecasts against the counts, and
Figure~\ref{fig:mae} each model's error with its interval.

\paragraph{Scores}
M4 has the lowest mean absolute error, root mean squared error and MASE of the four models
(Table~\ref{tab:scores}), and shares the lowest weighted interval score and Brier score with M4
without the flood index. Every model's MASE is above 1 because MASE divides by the error of ``same week
last year'' in 2016--2017, when QC recorded only 163 and 176 cases a year, against 366 to 1,033
a year from 2023 on; MASE therefore ranks the models but does not mean they lose to that naive
forecast in the scored years, where M4's error is below M0's. All models except M3 forecast too
low on average, by 3.8 to 5.3 cases a week, since a forecast made three weeks after the newest
count it sees lags behind each surge; M3's positive bias comes from its overshoots in 2018. M4's
ranges were wider than they needed to be: its 90\% range held the count in 96\% of weeks and
its 50\% range in 70\%, so they err on the side of caution. M0's 90\% range was too narrow,
holding the count in only 73\% of weeks.

\paragraph{Where the difference comes from}
Much of M3's error comes from 2018, when only 2015--2017 lay behind it: as the July outbreak
began, it forecast 1,065 cases for the week of 29~July to 4~August, which had 58
(Figure~\ref{fig:forecasts}), and its mean absolute error for 2018 was 50.4 cases a week against
M4's 7.4. This is why the interval is wide. M4 also missed less than M3 in five of the other six
scored years, by 0.7 to 11.9 cases a week; M3 missed less only in 2022 (2.6 against 2.9). M4 was
not the best model every year: among the four, it had the lowest error in 2018, 2019, 2023 and
2026, M0 in 2024 and 2025, and M3 in 2022. The hardest year was 2026, with the backtest's largest
weekly count (223 cases) and every model's highest error but M3's.

\paragraph{The flood test}
Adding the flood exposure index did not make M4 more accurate: the mean difference in absolute
error between M4 and M4 without the index was $-0.15$ cases a week, with a 95\% interval of
$-0.50$ to $0.21$, and the 4- and 13-week blocks agree. The scores of the forecast distribution
say the same (Table~\ref{tab:scores}). Over these years, then, the flood maps earn their place in
Layer~2, not in the city forecast. M4 did beat M1, the same-week average a health office can
compute by hand, by 1.84 cases a week ($-3.28$ to $-0.25$; Diebold--Mariano $p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{Checks}\label{sec:results-checks}
All three findings survive every check in Table~\ref{tab:robustness}. With a one-week guard M4's
lead over M3 shrinks to 6.79 cases a week, and with a three-week guard it grows to 10.64,
because M3's error grows faster with the guard (12.96, 15.59 and 17.51 cases a week at one, two
and three weeks) than M4's (6.17, 6.70 and 6.87). Scored over all 454 weeks, COVID-19 years
included, M4's mean absolute error is 5.65 against M3's 12.90, and M4 and M4 without the index
again have the lowest weighted interval and Brier scores.

\paragraph{Breach probabilities}
Of the 247 weeks of 2022--2026, 38 breached the epidemic threshold. M4's breach probabilities
had the lowest Brier score (0.101, a skill of 0.34 over always quoting the usual breach rate),
against 0.135 for M3, 0.127 for M1 and 0.148 for M0 (Table~\ref{tab:scores}). They were also the
more reliable (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}): M4's steps lie close to the diagonal, and the weeks
it gave a 27\% to 55\% chance breached 17 times in 35, while the weeks M3 gave a 36\% to 84\%
chance breached only 11 times in 34.

Turned into alarms, M4's probabilities were precise but missed most outbreaks. The cut-off with
the best F1 score on the first half of 2022--2026 was a 33\% chance; on the second half, from
the week of 12~May 2024, M4 raised 11 alarms, 8 of them in breach weeks, but these covered only 8
of the 29 breach weeks and 4 of the 14 outbreak episodes (95\% interval 8\% to 58\%), each before
surveillance would have shown it, by a median of 2.5 weeks. M1 and M3 caught 9 episodes each,
and M0 13, with four to eight times as many alarms (48 to 87), about one in four of them right.
The cut-off was chosen when breaches were rare, 9 in the first 123 weeks, and proved too strict
for the larger outbreaks from mid-2024 on, with 29 in the next 124. The full precision--recall
curve shows the trade-off: over all of 2022--2026, a 20\% cut-off would have flagged 74\% of the
breach weeks with 41\% of M4's alarms right, and M4's best possible F1 score, 0.56, is above M1's
(0.41) and M3's (0.34). That best cut-off is chosen in hindsight, so these figures describe the
curve rather than a forecast's performance.

\paragraph{Observed rain}
On IMERG, none of the four storms reached the five-year rain (Table~\ref{tab:layer2}): each fell
between the no-flood floor and the five-year level, at return periods of 1.0 to 2.2 years.
Kristine, 195.1~mm in three days, and the July 2025 floods, 191.0~mm, put 13.8\% and 13.1\% of
QC's residents in flooded zones, 425,192 and 404,966 people. The August 2026 habagat exposed 124,765
(4.0\%), and Carina 1,414: its 120.0~mm sat on the floor of 119.7~mm, the satellite
under-reading discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps the two 2024
storms. At full coverage, the six-day July 2025 flood needs 542,106 doxycycline capsules, 130,719
children's courses and 19,169 amoxicillin tablets. For Kristine's one-day flood the doxycycline
need depends on the share $w$ of exposed adults with a wound or mucosal exposure: 569,182
capsules at $w = 0$, 1,707,544 at 0.5 and 2,845,907 at 1. These are stock-planning figures, not
prescriptions.

\paragraph{Flagged barangays}
At the 25\% line, the July 2025 floods flagged 17 barangays, 5 of which reports named as flooded
(29\%), and so flagged 5 of the 14 named (36\%). Kristine flagged 17, 3 of them named (18\%),
and 3 of the 8 named (38\%). Carina and the August 2026 floods flagged none, though reports named
12 and 17. At the 50\% line only one barangay was flagged for July 2025 and two for Kristine.

\paragraph{Cut-off and duration tests}
Moving the continuous-exposure cut-off to one or three days, or the flood's duration by a day,
changed no storm's stock except Kristine's. With a one-day cut-off, or a two-day flood, all of
Kristine's exposed adults become high risk, and its doxycycline need is the $w = 0$ figure,
569,182 capsules. The other floods last three to six days, so their adults stay high risk at any
of these settings, and a flood of up to seven days needs one weekly dose per adult.

\paragraph{Forecast rain}
A day ahead, the forecasts put Carina and Kristine between ERA5's five- and 25-year rain, 224.4
and 233.1~mm, exposing 25\% and 26\% of residents, far more than their observed-rain runs (0.05\%
and 13.8\%). For Carina the gap more likely reflects the satellite under-reading the storm
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) than the forecast overshooting. A one-day forecast would thus have prepared Carina's
stock, about 1.0~million capsules for a long flood and 3.0~million for a one-day flood at
$w = 0.5$, but for many more barangays than reports named: of the 70 it flagged, 9 were named, 9
of the 12 named. Two and three days ahead the forecasts were smaller: three days ahead,
Carina's exposed 6.5\% of residents. For the July 2025 floods every lead fell short of the
observed run: 127.6~mm a day ahead (4.7\%), and three days ahead 93.2~mm, below ERA5's floor, so
no one. For the August 2026 habagat all three leads came close to the observed run (4.0\% to
8.0\% against 4.0\%).

\subsection{Comparison with the Benchmark}\label{sec:results-benchmark}

On the benchmark's own national data, our seasonal naive forecast matched all eight of its
published scores, and our Prophet came within 4.4\% of the published mean absolute error (24.73
against 23.68), but our hybrid did not reproduce the published gain: its error was 24.60 against
16.72, and its one-sided Diebold--Mariano test against Prophet gave $p = 0.49$ against the
published 0.043 \parencite{latigay2026}. The two evaluations also differ in kind. The benchmark
scored 18 national forecasts, two weeks ahead from nine rolling origins, and tested the hybrid
against Prophet with a one-sided test; we scored 349 weekly one-week-ahead forecasts of QC's
cases and set a two-sided 95\% interval on the difference in mean absolute error. QC's weekly
counts are also far smaller than the national ones, so our errors cannot be set beside the
paper's. Our result compares M4 with our reproduction of M3, refitted every week on what the
guard lets it see, not with the published accuracy.
